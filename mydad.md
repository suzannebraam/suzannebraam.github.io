# Tribute to My Dad 

Good morning. My name is Suzanne and I am Frank’s youngest daughter. On behalf of our family, we want to thank you for coming to celebrate Dad’s life with us. 

My Dad loved to tell stories, and I am going to share a few of the stories that Dad shared with us to allow you to get to know this wonderful man a bit better. 

Eph 6: 2 says *“Honour your father and mother so that it may go well with you and you may enjoy a long life on the earth”.* I pray that this tribute honours his memory and that you get to see a bit of the character of this man we all love so much.

Frank Peter Klassen was born Jan 17, 1928 in a village called *Zentral*, Russia. He was the 5th child of six children. When Frank was one year old, the family had to flee their home and they settled in the *Molotchna Mennonite Settlement* in the village of *Friedensdorf.* Dad grew up on the family farm. 

### Dad always told me that he was “spoiled rotten” and not disciplined very often. 

He had epilepsy from a young age and his seizures scared the family so much that this mom always told the other kids to “let Frankie have what he wants” because he has “hinfallenkrankheit” which translates to “falling down sickness”.

### Dad had a rambunctious nature and due to his being “spoiled rotten”, he often pulled pranks on the teacher and other kids at school. 

He had a “special place of honour” right in front of the teacher’s desk at the front of the classroom so she could keep an eye on him.
One teacher in particular was a woman who liked to stretch out her feet so that they were under Dad’s desk.
One day, Dad decided that he’d had enough of this, so he tied the teacher’s shoe laces together, and she when she tried to stand up, she fell over. 

*Although the classroom was arranged with the little kids in front and the bigger kids in the back, Dad never left the front row during his school career.* 

### Life was busy on the farm and there were many ways for curious boys to get into trouble. 

Dad loved to tell the story about how his brothers dared him to get into the pen with the wild bull, thinking that “Little Frankie” wouldn’t do it. But “Little Frankie” did, much to his brothers’ horror. Little Frankie armed himself with a hammer and struck the charging bull on the head to avoid being gored by the horns\!

*Little Frankie survived his childhood with a few cuts and bruises, the respect of his brothers, and the loss of the nickname “Little Frankie”.*  

### Then the war came and his childhood was gone forever. 

Dad was 11 when WWII started and 17 when it ended in 1945. Dad served in the army when he was 16 and his job was to load the cannons because his small hands could easily fit inside the barrels. He was liberated by the British and spent 2 weeks in a POW camp. He survived on 3 crackers and a cup of tea a day. 

### His father told him what when the war ended to “not let anyone know where you are from”. 

Dad secretly burned his identification papers that showed that he was born in Russia. When dad filled out new papers, he added a 0 to the month, saying his birthday was in October rather than January, making him 15 instead of 16\. The camp let the youngest go first, so he left the camp earlier and made his way to his Aunt and Uncle in Germany. He later applied for sponsorship to Canada.  

### Dad once wrote down a bit of his life story and this is how he described this time in his life:

*“I came to Canada at 19 years of age. I lost my family at the age of 16, so I had to stand on my own feet. In Canada, I had to work for one year on a farm near Owen Sound; that was my first home in Canada. These people treated me well and they wanted me to stay longer after the year was up, because I worked very hard. They even wanted to buy me a farm if I stayed.”*   

Dad loved his surrogate family, the Monkmans and he loved being back on a farm. When Mr. Monkman bought a brand new tractor for the farm, Dad taught him how to use it.   
### After the year was up, Dad moved to Kitchener and began taking English classes at night school. 

There he met a shy girl named Frieda Helfinger who wasn’t very interested in him at the time. Frieda let Frank drive her home after their classes in his awesome car. Dad told me that when he asked Mom to marry him, my Mom’s brother Joe took Dad aside and said, “If you don’t treat my sister right, you will answer to me”. 

Dad said that he was thinking “you can’t do anything to me because I can run a lot faster than you\! 

### Fortunately, they never came to blows, and Mom and Dad married on June 16, 1951\. 

They soon had a house full of girls, and a thriving business. Dad owned a construction company and was a plasterer by trade. He also owned and maintained several apartment buildings. He was always busy.

### His love of the country remained and in the early 80’s he bought a property out in Bamberg and had a small hobby farm with a large pond, a huge garden, and a handful of animals. 

- Dad and Mom loved this property and Dad approached each project with gusto.
- He built the house with a large deck, a 2 story barn and an enormous garage.
- He dug a pond, built a deck with ladders and a diving board, constructed a bridge across the creek, and designed chicken coops, feeding troughs, and fences.
- His industrial saw ran every day and the workshop’s piles of sawdust provided endless cat litter for the batch of kittens born each season. 

### Years later, the size and the amount of work became too much and he and Mom moved to the last home that he built on Hunter’s Place in Waterloo. 

It was the “only plaster house on the street”. As their health began to decline, Mom and Dad eventually moved to Parkwood and then SunnySide home in Kitchener.   
He leaves behind a legacy of 62 years of marriage, 5 children, 14 grandchildren, and 7 great grandchildren.

### This is only a part of Frank’s story. 

It’s hard to condense 85 years into a few minutes but to end off, I wanted to share a bit more about what kind of man he was. 

## Dad was a man of his word, a man of his hands, and a man of his faith.

### First, Dad was a man of his word. 

Dad believed that your words revealed your character and trust needed to be earned. When it became too hard for my Dad to complete a renovation, we hired a renovation company to finish some work at my parents’ home. My friend, who works for this company told me later that when the initial contract was signed, Dad wouldn’t put down a deposit to start the contract, but would only pay after the work was done. 

*He said, “I won’t pay you upfront, but I will shake your hand, and give you my word”.*
Because of his character and reputation as a builder, I think he was the only customer who had work done without an initial deposit. 

### Dad loved words and he loved to tell stories.

Every family gathering always included a time for him to make a speech. He was proud of us and he loved to brag about Mom, “Frieda is the best looking lady in the room, Frieda’s cooking is better than anyone else’s”, “My girls are so smart”. 

When Mom was diagnosed with Alzheimers, Dad became her primary care giver and looked after her by himself for several years. He promised her that when it was impossible for her to stay at home that they would move somewhere together and they did. 

### Dad kept his promise. Dad was a man of his word.

## Second, Dad was a man of his hands. 

He was hard working and handy.

### He showed love by serving. He loved to make things, fix things, and take care of things for his girls. 

Growing up, Dad didn’t really “play” with us, but he would make things for us. 

One summer Dad made me a slingshot and showed me how to fling stones at a target on a tree. 
I helped him build a tree house and a doll house. 

Later, when I grew up had a house of my own, Dad was the one who came over and made repairs. 
He constructed shelves in the garage, built shoe racks in the closets, and fixed warped studs in the walls. 

He helped all of us with our projects. He helped the neighbours with their projects too. 

Dad was the person in the neighbourhood that everyone borrowed tools from because he had **every tool** for **every job**. 

Dad was the only resident at Sunnyside who brought a toolbox in with him just in case he needed to fix something.  

### Dad worked hard and he taught us to work hard. He didn’t “hang out” with us, he put us to work. 

Growing up I used to hate all the chores and projects that we had to do. 

### But as an adult I realize that he related to us through work and when he asked us to do something for him he was allowing us to love him. Dad was a man of his hands.

## And finally, Dad was a man of his faith. 

Dad came from a lineage of men and women who loved and served God. 
- Dad’s father, my Opa, was imprisoned and killed by the communist regime for being a Christian.
- His mother, my Oma, was a “prayer warrior” and she had a vision of Jesus meeting her before she passed away.
- Dad made a promise to his parents that he would never leave the faith growing up.
- He made WK his home church and was loyal to the body for more than 60 years.
- He raised all of us in the faith.

### Although his generation didn’t talk about their faith, when my mom became ill, Dad began to rely on and share his faith.   

**Romans 8:28**: And we know that in **all** **things** God **works** for the good of those who love Him, who have been called according to His purpose.

Through this last year of dealing with illness, we have watched our Dad transform from an independent person to a person dependent on God. 
- He became generous, thankful, and humble.
- He shared his faith with others gave thanks to God when some detail fell into place.
- He even reconciled some relationships that had been broken for years.
- He asked for prayer and he prayed for others out loud.
- As long as he was able, he would go visit my mom every day and read the Bible and do daily devotions.

### We watched him live out his faith.

## Frank was a man of his word, a man of his hands, and a man of his faith. 

- He remained faithful to my mom for 62 years.
- He raised 5, hardworking, responsible, and feisty girls.
- He called us his biggest blessing.
- He was proud his grandkids too.
   
### He was good man and we will all miss him. 

So, in closing, I want to say, “Enjoy your family reunion, Dad. I wish we could hear your speech”.

Thank you.
